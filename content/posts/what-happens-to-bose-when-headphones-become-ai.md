<p class="has-drop-cap wp-block-paragraph">Today, I’m talking with Lila Snyder, who is the CEO of Bose.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">You certainly know Bose — it’s one of the most famous brands in all of consumer tech. The company started 60 years ago selling speakers to consumers, and its focus on research and development has led it to be a leader in both car audio and noise-canceling headphones.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Lila was <a href="https://www.theverge.com/23482627/bose-ceo-lila-snyder-interview-headphones-noise-control-apple-airpods-bluetooth-cars">last on the show about four years ago</a>, and since then she’s dramatically rearchitected the company. The focus on research is still there, but she’s <a href="https://www.bose.com/pressroom/bose-pro-announcement">sold off Bose Professional</a>, acquired the high-end audio brands <a href="https://www.theverge.com/2024/11/19/24300618/bose-mcintosh-group-audio-acquisition">McIntosh Labs and Sonus faber</a>, and, perhaps most importantly, started licensing all that core Bose technology to other companies.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That means that while you can still go buy a Bose soundbar or Bose headphones, the company is also turning into a B2B business, and you can <a href="https://www.theverge.com/tech/966022/skullcandy-cruser-1080-anc-bose-tuning-noise-cancelling-wireless-headphones">get Bose technology in Skullcandy headphones</a> and Epson projectors, among many other products.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That big change in the business comes with a big change in structure. Becoming a software company in addition to a product company comes with a whole new set of challenges, and Lila and I talked about how it’s all working out. </p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I also wanted to know if this is all a reaction to the potential rise of AI wearables — products that might end up looking a lot like headphones, and which need all kinds of audio processing to enable voice input.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Is Bose just trying to sell tech to a range of new companies that might disrupt headphones entirely? Or does Lila have a plan for a headphones market that was already disrupted when phone vendors like Apple and Samsung made their own products like AirPods and Galaxy Buds work better than the competition? </p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We got deep on this, and Lila was pretty open about the challenges and the opportunities here. As <em>Decoder </em>listeners know, I’m a huge audio nerd, so I loved every minute of this conversation. I hope you do too.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Okay: Bose CEO Lila Snyder. Here we go.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><em>This interview has been lightly edited for length and clarity.&nbsp;</em></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Lila Snyder, you&#8217;re CEO of Bose. Welcome back to </strong><strong><em>Decoder</em></strong><strong>.</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Thank you so much.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>I am really excited to talk to you. It&#8217;s been four years since you were last on the show, which seems entirely too long.</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>A lot of things have happened. Bose has bought some companies. You&#8217;ve sold some divisions. The idea of what headphones are is largely the same in one important way, but maybe going to be very different in another important way. How are you thinking about Bose today?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We are having a lot of fun in what I would describe as our second chapter of the transformation of Bose since I joined. In some ways, many things are the same. We&#8217;re still making amazing products for our customers. We&#8217;re still excited about bringing that new technology forward in both our consumer business and our automotive business. But I would say two things fundamentally have changed as we&#8217;ve thought about growth for Bose for the future.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For 60 years, it was all Bose, and we&#8217;re now a multi-brand company. So as you said, we&#8217;ve made a couple of acquisitions. One of those was the McIntosh brand, a legendary 75-year-old audiophile brand that we absolutely love, and also Sonus faber, which is an Italian craftsmanship heritage brand that&#8217;s also been around for over 40 years. And so that brought us two things: from one brand to multiple brands, but also into the luxury audio space.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The second change for us is that we&#8217;ve always been an amazing products company where we developed technology through research and a lot of development cycles, and all of that technology went into Bose products. And today what&#8217;s different is we&#8217;re still doing that, and we&#8217;ve added a technology business we call Audio Tech, where we&#8217;re taking that technology and starting to put it into other places.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The big insight for us was that sound is really important. It&#8217;s getting more important, we think, given everything that&#8217;s going on in the world around us. There are so many products and devices out there that are using sound — whether that&#8217;s audio, voice, or even noise-canceling, where those things are incredibly relevant — but they&#8217;re not products that Bose would make. They&#8217;re not targeted at our customer set. But we really have a strong belief in bringing great audio to as many places as we can.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>The last time we talked, you were in the middle of becoming a more functional company. In particular, you had said the last step was the engineering organization, which is really the heart of Bose. It had been very divisional, very organized into products. You were centralizing that.</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Having an audio tech division that sells core technology feels like a very natural offshoot of that. &#8220;We&#8217;re going to have a big central engineering team. It&#8217;s going to feed into our products and other products,” and then you can abstract that a little bit and sell it to other people. Who are the customers for the audio tech division?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">So it&#8217;s a B2B2C business, if you will. So our customers are large companies that are putting products into the market. So I&#8217;ll give you a couple of examples. We&#8217;ve partnered with Motorola on a series of products from earbuds to making the phone itself sound better.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">So Motorola is a good example. We&#8217;re partnered with Xreal. They&#8217;re making pretty awesome XR glasses, right? So that&#8217;s an experience where sound really matters a lot. We&#8217;ve had a lot of fun partnering with them. You&#8217;re starting to see Bose technology show up in a lot of different verticals, from mobile to compute, to other people&#8217;s headphones and audio products for sure.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We just released, with a partner called Sena, a motorcycle helmet communications device. And we&#8217;re excited about all the places we&#8217;re bringing our technology. It&#8217;s actually creating a broader aperture for how we think about invention and research at Bose as well, which is really exciting.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>I want to come to that because that seems like a big change. And the reason I asked about customers is that having a new set of customers really changes the focus of the company. A lot of us are familiar with audio brands in particular partnering with device makers, partnering with automakers.</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Yeah.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Maybe it&#8217;s just a logo. I think you have been very proud that Bose has not been just a logo in many places, but for a lot of people it&#8217;s just a logo. What&#8217;s the difference between, okay, there&#8217;s Xreal glasses powered by Bose with a Bose logo on the side, and selling the technology without the Bose logo? Or does a Bose logo always come with it?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When you said this is new for Bose, in a way, yes, but in a way it&#8217;s exactly what we&#8217;ve been doing in automotive for over 40 years. And so we do have a model for doing this inside the company, and it&#8217;s just broadening that thinking into new spaces. First and foremost, we&#8217;re selling technology, and the technology is the key. And then in some cases, we&#8217;re also selling the brand. So you might get technology in a product without a Sound by Bose or Powered by Bose logo on it, or you might get the logo as well, but you&#8217;ll never get the logo without the tech, if that makes sense.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Some of our partners are interested in the brand, some are not. Sometimes we&#8217;re interested in having our brand connected to their brand, and sometimes we&#8217;re not. And so that&#8217;s part of the discussion we have with each of our customers, about whether it makes sense for them and for us. And so when you see it, know that it&#8217;s been really thoughtful, but it&#8217;s also attached to a lot of great technology that&#8217;s inside that product.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Tell me about those conversations. Is it cheaper to take the brand? Is it more expensive to take the brand? This is one of those places where Bose has maybe a bigger, more famous brand than maybe some of the customers. How are you thinking about that, and how does the cost change?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As you would expect, the technology has a licensing fee to it, and so does the brand. We&#8217;ve invested over 60 years in our brand. We&#8217;re incredibly proud of it. It is, I think, one of the most iconic brands in the world, full stop, and certainly within the audio space. And so we are incredibly careful about where we put the brand, and it certainly isn&#8217;t free when you put it on a product.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Right next to that is McIntosh. As you know, I grew up a total stereo nerd, just reading the magazines, dreaming about having the blue meters in my house someday. Still working on it. I&#8217;m going to get there. I did have a Jeep with a McIntosh sound system several years ago. The blue meters were&#8230; It was a very fake Android app that ran on a very slow Android infotainment system, but I still loved it.</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>That&#8217;s a brand that has a real halo. You can&#8217;t make cheaper McIntosh products. That&#8217;s not good for that brand.</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">No way.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>You need to stay way up there. How are you thinking about that relationship to Bose, which is much more of a premium mass market brand?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We think of them as two completely separate brands. And then think about Sonus faber as yet another third brand, all of which are targeting really passionate fans of those brands for what they stand for. So when we thought about getting into the luxury space and acquiring those two brands, we spent a lot of time saying, &#8220;What is it that we&#8217;re going to do internally to protect the brands?&#8221;&nbsp;</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We do not want to Bose-ify anything that is happening in those brands. And so we&#8217;ve set it up as a separate business unit. We have a luxury audio business unit that houses those two brands. We are very careful. As you said, we have no intention of bringing products down-market. We believe both markets are exciting. We&#8217;ve been in that premium audio space for a long time.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When we looked at what we&#8217;re calling luxury audio, we had two really important insights, I think. One is that as we&#8217;re doing our own technology work, there&#8217;s a lot of really exciting technology we develop that just isn&#8217;t right for a premium audio product. It&#8217;s right for something that sits at a higher level. We just didn&#8217;t have anywhere to put it. And that was true on the consumer product side, and it&#8217;s certainly true on the automotive side where we felt like we were bumping up against what you could put in a premium product, and the ceiling of what we wanted to do was much higher.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">So the first reason we&#8217;re excited about it is that it sort of unleashes the creativity of our engineers to think about what you could do at those higher price points with just a different type of customer, a different type of experience. So we were excited on the technology side.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We were equally excited, I think, about this idea of luxury. If you look at the luxury market writ large across all categories, what you find is that audio is really underrepresented compared to the role luxury plays in automotive, hospitality and travel, fashion, or leather goods. And when we really sat back and thought about it, given how much music is a passion point for people, it didn&#8217;t make sense to us.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We think there&#8217;s a huge opportunity to attract new customers to the category if the experience feels more like a luxury experience. And so when you think about those products, it&#8217;s certainly about how they sound, but you went right to it. It&#8217;s the iconic design, it&#8217;s the artistry, it&#8217;s the craftsmanship. All of our luxury products, whether it&#8217;s McIntosh or Sonus faber, are handmade. They are crafted one by one for you as the customer. And there&#8217;s something incredibly special about that.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>These weren&#8217;t your decisions, so I feel like I can be a little bit rude about them. When I saw McIntosh was licensing to Stellantis to be in Jeeps, I thought, &#8220;This company has to be desperate for money. That&#8217;s not actually a match.&#8221; It was fun that I had a Jeep with blue meters in it. It was great.</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>But I thought to myself, &#8220;This is a total mismatch, and they should be in Maybachs,&#8221; or whatever it is, whatever luxury sector that you want to be in, because that&#8217;s the brand. Was this just opportunistic for you that this was a company that needed the help, that was in trouble, that you could go and you could push your products upmarket without having to take the Bose brand there? Or was there some other opportunity that presented itself to you?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We weren&#8217;t actually shopping for brands first. The first thing was, strategically, we saw the opportunity in the luxury space. We saw an opportunity to create something new around luxury audio, and we were really excited about that.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">So once we had made the strategic decision to go there, we had a lot of conversations about buying a brand or creating a brand. But the reality is, in luxury, at those price points, history and heritage are part of what you&#8217;re buying. And so we sort of intuitively knew we wanted to find a brand or brands that really had that strong history that we felt like we could build on top of.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We thought those were both brands that had incredibly passionate fans, and we saw an opportunity for us to take them to a new level. So that was how we ended up acquiring the McIntosh group.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Bringing in their engineering organization, they have a lot of technology. How integrated has that become over time, or are you leaving them alone?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We&#8217;ve brought it into our engineering organization, but we&#8217;ve also kept a lot of the aspects a little bit separate. So probably the best example of that is Bose has a Bose sound. Everybody knows that. You buy a Bose product… You buy one, the headphones I&#8217;m wearing, the speaker in your house, your car. There is a sound signature to brands that consumers expect. That comes from the engineers.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The engineers are tuning products to sound like they should. And so we&#8217;re very careful on the engineering side to protect the sound signature of McIntosh. What we don&#8217;t want is for you to hear a McIntosh product or a Sonus faber product and imagine that it sounds different. So that&#8217;s one.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Initially — as you do with, I think, any acquisition — you want to be careful to protect what you bought. We&#8217;ve integrated it, yes, but we have a separate team that is doing the engineering for luxury, and we&#8217;re slowly adding Bose technology to it where it makes sense. So there are things, for example, on the Bose side where we can be incredibly helpful in terms of the software aspects, the connectivity, those sorts of things. Those are things that we do really well, and they&#8217;re not the most important part of those products, but they have to work. There are aspects that we&#8217;ve started to add into the products, and you&#8217;ll see more of that over time.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>I&#8217;m really curious about that luxury aspect. The knock on high-end audio for years, and I&#8217;m not saying this is McIntosh, but the knock on high-end audio for years is that it&#8217;s the same stuff, all dressed up fancy. There are headphones that are famous for having weights in them to feel heavier even though there are commodity parts inside.</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>This is one of those places where you could just say, “We&#8217;re just going to make more expensive versions of the same thing.” And some buyers are totally price-insensitive, in that they will always just buy the most expensive thing. Then there&#8217;s, “We&#8217;re going to make it worth it. We&#8217;re going to make it worth 10X the price of whatever Bluetooth speakers are at Best Buy.”&nbsp;</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Where are you falling on that? Because it&#8217;s very tempting, especially in luxury, to just make it expensive.</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The value comes from the sound. You should come visit us at Bose in Framingham and listen to a McIntosh system, a Sonus faber system. They&#8217;re incredible. They&#8217;re not anything like what you would get at a Best Buy in a premium product from Bose or anyone else. And that&#8217;s incredibly special.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Part of that is the technology itself. Part of that is that it&#8217;s more analog, so it has a different sound signature to it. It&#8217;s got a lot more power behind it because that&#8217;s what McIntosh is known for, and that&#8217;s what you need to power a Sonus faber set of speakers. You&#8217;re also paying for design. In a way, it&#8217;s a little bit of art.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We just launched a new integrated amplifier, the <a href="https://www.gramophone.com/blog/review-mcintosh-ma-2375-all-tube-integrated-amplifier">MA2375</a>, for McIntosh. I&#8217;m so excited about it. It&#8217;s sort of got that retro look with the vacuum tubes. It is a design piece. I want to sit it on my desk. I want it to be a part of the decor. You would say the same thing on the Sonus faber side. It&#8217;s like buying an incredible piece of Italian furniture that has been handmade with natural materials and just feels like art that also sounds beautiful.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">So there&#8217;s more to the value proposition in the luxury space. It&#8217;s true in the premium space too. Design really matters, color matters, feel, touch, comfort, all those things matter in addition to the sound. I think it&#8217;s just even that much more elevated in the luxury space.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>I&#8217;m really curious about all the different sets of new customers you&#8217;re describing. There&#8217;s the B2B business, which is growing in audio tech. Then there are luxury customers who have a lot of money, but they&#8217;re fickle. And particularly in high-end AV — and I&#8217;m a high-end AV nerd through and through — convincing people it&#8217;s worth it because they can hear it has always been the hardest problem.</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Maybe the most fun problem, because you can convince a lot of people they&#8217;re hearing a lot of things that aren&#8217;t there. You can sell expensive cables at a hundred times the price of the regular cables, even if they don&#8217;t do anything different. This is a lot of new customers for Bose. Do you think the B2B business is going to distract your core technology roadmap? How are you holding that all together?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It is more complex for sure. It is a broader mission, and I think we&#8217;re ready for it. When I was here last time, we were in the middle of the transformation that I would say was solidifying the foundation of Bose, and now we&#8217;re building on top of that. We&#8217;ve built an amazing marketing and brand infrastructure that we&#8217;re now using to power three brands instead of one, but a lot of that capability can be put in the direction of all three.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">So the key is real clarity about who the customer is, which we&#8217;ve done a lot of work on the Bose brand for sure. We talk about the music lover for Bose, but we&#8217;ve done the same work for McIntosh and the same work for Sonus faber so that we are one thousand percent clear about who the customer is and how we want to talk to that customer.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">On the kind of products side of our company, I think we’re really clear about what the mission is for each one of those brands. On the technology side, interestingly, as we become more of a B2B technology company, it&#8217;s actually creating advantages back to the Bose brand and our luxury business as well. And what I mean by that is that it&#8217;s caused us to completely rethink the way that we do research and we develop our tech.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In the past, if you think about Bose developing products, we were developing purpose-built technology for a pair of headphones or a car audio system. And I think last time we talked about the tech franchises. We care a lot about noise cancellation. We care about immersive, lifelike audio, and we care about this idea of hearing what you want. And those are all experiences that are built on some basic building blocks of technology that we&#8217;ve been developing for a long time.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But because we were developing them for our own products, we&#8217;ve had to do a complete shift in the way that we think about it so that we&#8217;re now developing, from the ground up, those building blocks. Now we&#8217;re able to say, with common platforms, common algorithms, AI — think of them as sort of AI units — we can create different experiences for our own products, and we can port that same technology through the same platform tech into other people&#8217;s products.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What that&#8217;s creating is a lot of efficiency at the research and development level. It&#8217;s creating more breadth because now that we&#8217;re thinking about things like noise cancellation for different spaces, spatial audio, or hearing what you want, it is actually creating more innovation and more invention at the core. It&#8217;s making us faster because we&#8217;ve made these building blocks a lot more consumable for our B2B customers; it makes us faster at bringing our own products to market, too.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>I think this is a great place to ask the Decoder questions because you&#8217;re describing the endpoint of a pretty big culture and structure shift at Bose that you&#8217;ve been architecting for several years. So how is Bose structured today?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">There&#8217;s a business unit structure, there&#8217;s a functional structure, and we are living in what I would describe as a hybrid. So for the core functions that matter deeply to what we deliver, like engineering, marketing, and supply chain, those are global functions that support all of our businesses. But because we&#8217;ve added new customer sets and really two new businesses with luxury and audio tech, we&#8217;re also running four business units. So those four business units have business unit presidents, and they draw on the global functions for the work that they need.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>How many people is Bose today?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">About 2,500.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>And you&#8217;re mostly in Framingham, or are you all over the place?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We&#8217;re global. All over. Yep.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>You said you&#8217;re a hybrid. How&#8217;s that split between engineering, sales, all that sort of thing?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I don&#8217;t actually think about it that way, but the one thing that I do know — sort of the most important statistic to me — is that half of our employees are engineers.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>You&#8217;re describing AWS, you&#8217;re describing Amazon. You&#8217;ve modularized a bunch of components inside the company; now you can build new products faster. This is really great. Every company that goes through this transition ends up hiring more and more engineers over time because you just need to build more and more components. Has that been growing? Are you investing more in that kind of engineering?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We are for sure. I think it&#8217;s probably not unique to us. We&#8217;ve been in this transition of hardware long, long ago. It was just hardware, and then hardware and software, and then more and more software. And now as we&#8217;re building the technology pieces, those are truly just software products that are an algorithm- or AI-based product that we&#8217;re selling with.&nbsp;</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">You see that mixed shift continuing. We&#8217;re certainly seeing growth in our engineering ranks. And we&#8217;re getting a lot more productivity out of engineering as we start to have more sophisticated tools, as we use AI and other things to help with some of that development. So yes, growing. And I think more productivity per engineer, if you will, as we start to use more sophisticated tools to do the development.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>You said it&#8217;s pure software products, right? When you sell out to B2B, you&#8217;re just selling the algorithms. Do those feel protectable? This is one of the things that I think about all the time. What is the actual thing you&#8217;re selling, and how easily can it be copied by a Chinese firm that does not respect our intellectual property laws or something like that?</strong></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Yeah. I&#8217;m probably oversimplifying. We&#8217;re really selling three things. We&#8217;re definitely selling software, algorithms, et cetera. I&#8217;ll come back to your “how do you protect it?” question. One of the very special things about Bose is actually systems engineering, which is an underappreciated discipline in my view. So software&#8217;s great, but software has to run on hardware in our world. Ultimately, that software has to get delivered through something.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The magic at Bose, the secret sauce, has always been systems engineering and how you&#8217;re marrying together hardware and software elements for an experience that feels unified across the device and that&#8217;s optimized to provide the best experiences.
